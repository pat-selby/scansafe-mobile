## 1. Vision & Mission

### Vision Statement

A world where scanning a QR code is not an act of blind trust.

### Mission Statement

ScanSafe puts a deterministic, explainable phishing check between a QR code and the
person scanning it — running entirely on their phone, so protection never costs them
their privacy or depends on a network connection they may not have.

### Founder's Why

Patrick Selby is a cybersecurity undergraduate at Grambling State University who
investigated real phishing campaigns on his own campus and turned what he found into
detection rules. The rule set is not theoretical. Rule 13 exists because a SafeLinks
wrapper hid a malicious destination from students. Rule 14's `blob:` weighting exists
because blob URLs appeared in a real attack and never appear in a legitimate QR code.
Rule 22 exists because `ivoryrobinson94.wixsite.com/0ne-dr1ve` impersonated OneDrive
convincingly enough to work. This is a tool built by someone encoding what actually
happened to people he knows.

### Core Values

1. **Nothing leaves the device.** Privacy is not a feature to be traded for accuracy.
2. **Show the work.** A verdict without reasoning is asking for trust the product hasn't earned.
3. **Deterministic over clever.** Every result is reproducible and auditable. No black boxes.
4. **Calm over loud.** Fear is not a security control. Explanation is.
5. **Offline is the default, not the fallback.** Campus Wi-Fi fails; protection shouldn't.

### Strategic Pillars

1. **The engine is the product.** The 22-rule scorer is the research contribution and must be portable, testable, and provably correct against the published prototype.
2. **Explainability as differentiation.** Every competitor returns a verdict. None return a reason a student can check.
3. **Privacy as an architectural constraint, not a marketing claim.** The MVP has no backend, no accounts, and no analytics, so the claim is structurally true rather than promised.
4. **Research credibility as distribution.** The NSF write-up and measured false-positive rates are the reason anyone should trust this over a free app store scanner.

### Success Looks Like

**90 days:** Free on Google Play and the App Store. Engine verified at parity with the
Python prototype by an automated corpus. Used in at least one GSU security-awareness
session. False-positive rate on legitimate domains measured and published.

**6 months:** The Layer 7 agentic investigation module ships as an optional, clearly
labelled addition covering DNS-based pharming — an attack class heuristics cannot touch.
A published evaluation compares heuristics alone against heuristics plus the agent.
Adoption extends past Grambling.

## 2. User Research

### Primary Persona

**Jasmine, 20, junior at Grambling State University.** Scans QR codes several times a
week without thinking — dining hall menus, parking meters, event flyers, a professor's
handout. Her phone is usually near 20% battery and campus Wi-Fi drops constantly. She got
a phishing text about her student account two weeks ago and knows the threat is real, but
has no practical way to check a code before scanning it. She is not a security expert and
does not want to become one; she wants a straight answer in the two seconds she has.

### Secondary Personas

- **University IT and security-awareness staff.** Need a tool they can project during a
  training session and use to explain *why* a link is dangerous. The reasoning matters
  more to them than the verdict — a black-box red badge teaches nothing.
- **Security researchers.** Evaluating the tradeoff between deterministic heuristics and
  ML-based detection. They need reproducible scoring and access to the rule weights.
- **Privacy-conscious users.** Will not install a scanner that transmits every link they
  encounter, and correctly assume most free QR apps do exactly that.

### Jobs To Be Done

- When I see a QR code in public, I want to know where it actually leads, so I can decide
  whether to open it without gambling.
- When an app warns me about a link, I want to see the reasoning, so I can tell a genuine
  threat from a false alarm.
- When I'm offline or on bad Wi-Fi, I want the check to still work, so protection isn't
  conditional on connectivity.
- When I check a link, I don't want that check logged anywhere, so using a security tool
  doesn't create a new privacy problem.

### Pain Points

1. **QR codes are unreadable by design.** You cannot hover over a printed square. The URL
   is only visible after you've already arrived.
2. **Built-in camera scanners offer zero safety signal.** iOS and Android happily open
   whatever the code contains.
3. **Third-party scanners are the threat model.** Ad-supported, permission-hungry, and
   frequently uploading every scanned URL to a server.
4. **Cloud reputation checks need the network that campus Wi-Fi doesn't provide.**
5. **No existing tool explains itself.** A verdict with no reason is indistinguishable
   from a coin flip, so users learn to dismiss warnings.

### Current Alternatives & Competitive Landscape

| Alternative | What it does | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|
| iOS / Android built-in camera | Decodes and opens the link | No safety check at all |
| Free QR scanner apps | Decode, often with a "safety" claim | Ads, broad permissions, URLs commonly uploaded |
| Browser safe-browsing | Blocklist check after navigation | Only helps once the page has loaded; needs network |
| Enterprise email link rewriting | Wraps links for inspection | Not available to students; is itself a Rule 13 signal |

The gap: no tool checks the link **before** navigation, **without** a network, and
**shows its reasoning**. ScanSafe occupies exactly that intersection.

### Key Assumptions to Validate

1. Students will accept a two-step flow (scan, then read a verdict) instead of the
   instant open they're used to. **Highest risk.**
2. Plain-English findings actually change behaviour rather than being dismissed.
3. The 22-rule engine's false-positive rate on ordinary campus URLs is low enough to keep
   trust. Early evidence is mixed — `grambling.edu` itself currently scores SUSPICIOUS.
4. OpenCV decoding is fast and reliable enough on mid-range Android hardware to feel instant.

### User Journey Map

1. **Encounter** — Jasmine sees a QR code on a flyer offering a parking permit discount.
2. **Choose the safe path** — She opens ScanSafe rather than her camera. *(The hardest step; the app must be fast to open and obvious to use.)*
3. **Scan** — She points at the code. OpenCV locates and decodes it on-device in under a second.
4. **Verdict** — HIGH RISK appears with the score, before any page loads.
5. **Understand** — She reads: the domain resembles a brand it isn't, and the path contains a credential keyword.
6. **Decide** — She doesn't open it. Optionally taps "See details" to see the exact rules.
7. **Recall** — The scan sits in local history if she wants to show someone later.

## 3. Product Strategy

### Product Principles

1. **The app explains links; it never opens them.** No "open anyway" button, ever.
2. **Plain English first, technical detail one tap away.** Both layers always available, neither forced.
3. **Every finding names the pattern it actually found.** Generic warnings are banned.
4. **Zero network calls in the scan path.** Crash reporting is the only exception and never carries scan content.
5. **Colour is never the only signal.** Every verdict carries an icon and a word.

### Market Differentiation

Competitors sell a verdict. ScanSafe sells a reason. That difference is structural, not
cosmetic: because the engine is 22 deterministic rules rather than a model, every result
can be decomposed into the specific patterns that produced it — and that decomposition is
the user-facing product, not debug output.

The privacy position is equally structural. With no backend, no accounts, and no
analytics, "nothing leaves your device" is a property of the architecture rather than a
promise in a policy document.

### Magic Moment Design

**The moment:** The phone is pointed at a QR code on a flyer, and before any page can
load, the screen reads HIGH RISK — because the domain closely resembles a brand it isn't.

**What makes it land:** Speed (verdict before the page would have loaded), specificity
(it names the brand being impersonated), and the fact that the user was about to scan it
anyway. The realisation is not "this app works" — it's "I would have fallen for that."

**Engineering implications:** Decode-to-verdict must feel instantaneous, which is why
scoring is a pure function with no I/O. The verdict word must be the largest element on
screen. The impersonated brand must appear in the plain-English finding, not just the
technical layer.

### MVP Definition

Layers 1–6, fully offline:

1. Camera capture with permission handling
2. OpenCV preprocessing pipeline
3. OpenCV QR decode with multi-stage fallback
4. 22-rule URL risk scoring
5. Verdict display with dual-layer findings
6. Local scan history, 50-item cap

Plus manual URL entry as a fallback path, and the crash reporting required for a
responsible public store release.

### Explicitly Out of Scope

- **Layer 7 agentic investigation** (WHOIS, DNS-over-HTTPS pharming defence, Gemini reasoning) — deferred to v2 by design; it introduces the network dependency the MVP exists to avoid.
- **Accounts, sync, cloud history** — permanently out of scope, not deferred.
- **Product analytics or telemetry** — contradicts the core claim.
- **Payments or subscriptions** — the app is free.
- **Non-URL QR payloads** (Wi-Fi credentials, vCards, payment codes) — decoded and shown, but not scored. Out of scope for MVP scoring.
- **Browser extension or desktop version.**

### Feature Priority (MoSCoW)

**Must have:** OpenCV QR decode; 22-rule engine at parity with the prototype; verdict
screen with dual-layer findings; camera permission handling; manual URL entry; local
history with 50-item cap; privacy policy; store listings.

**Should have:** Torch toggle for low light; scan-from-image-file; haptic feedback on
verdict; empty and error states for every screen.

**Could have:** Share a result as text; rule reference screen explaining all 22 rules;
history search.

**Won't have (this release):** Layer 7 agent; accounts; analytics; widgets; wearable app.

### Core User Flows

1. **Scan flow (primary):** Open app → tap Scan a QR code → grant camera permission →
   frame the code → decode → verdict → optionally expand findings → back to home.
2. **Manual flow (fallback):** Open app → type or paste a link → Scan this link → verdict.
3. **History flow:** Open app → history icon → list of past scans → tap one → full result;
   swipe to delete; clear all behind confirmation.
4. **Permission-denied flow:** Camera denied → explanatory state with a settings deep link
   and a working manual-entry fallback. The app must remain useful without the camera.

### Success Metrics

- Decode-to-verdict latency under 500ms on mid-range Android hardware.
- Parity corpus passing 100% against the Python prototype.
- False-positive rate on a legitimate-domain corpus measured and published; target under 5%.
- Crash-free session rate above 99.5%.
- At least one documented security-awareness session using the app.

### Risks

| Risk | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Users default to the built-in camera out of habit | Fatal to adoption | Make launch-to-scan under two taps; pursue campus-level promotion rather than app store discovery |
| False positives erode trust — `grambling.edu` currently scores SUSPICIOUS | High | Treat as a release blocker; measure against a legitimate-domain corpus and retune Rules 8 and 15 before launch |
| OpenCV plugin instability across platforms | High | Isolate behind a `QrDecoder` interface so the decoder can be swapped without touching the app |
| iOS build unverifiable on a Windows machine | Medium | Keep every layer platform-neutral; treat iOS as build-and-test-on-borrowed-Mac before submission |
| Solo developer with a full course load | Medium | Phase the roadmap so each phase ships something demoable on its own |

## 4. Brand Strategy

### Positioning Statement

For students and staff who scan QR codes in daily life, ScanSafe is a QR scanner that
checks where a code really goes before you open it — unlike built-in camera scanners that
open anything, and unlike free scanner apps that upload every link you scan, ScanSafe
runs entirely on your phone and shows you exactly why it reached its verdict.

### Brand Personality

The careful friend who checks things for you. Calm, unhurried, never alarmist. Confident
because it shows its work, not because it raises its voice. It assumes the person on the
other end is capable of understanding the reasoning if you take the time to explain it.

### Voice & Tone Guide

**Always:** Plain language. Second person. Short sentences. Name the specific pattern
detected. Explain the limits of what was checked.

**Never:** Fear as a substitute for explanation. Security jargon in the plain-English
layer. Generic warnings that could apply to any URL. Exclamation marks. Blame directed at
the user for scanning something.

| Situation | Say | Don't say |
|---|---|---|
| High risk | "This link isn't going where it says. The domain closely resembles 'paypal' but isn't it." | "⚠️ DANGER! Malicious link detected!" |
| Safe | "Nothing here matched a known phishing pattern. That checks the link itself, not the page it leads to." | "You're safe!" |
| Suspicious | "Some warning signs. If this asks you to sign in, go to the site directly instead." | "This might be dangerous, be careful." |
| Decode failure | "The camera couldn't read that code. Try moving a little closer." | "Error: decode failed." |
| Permission denied | "ScanSafe needs the camera to read QR codes. You can still check links by typing them below." | "Camera permission required." |

### Messaging Framework

- **Primary message:** Know where a QR code goes before you open it.
- **Supporting message 1:** It runs on your phone. Nothing you scan is sent anywhere.
- **Supporting message 2:** Every verdict comes with the reasoning behind it.
- **Supporting message 3:** Built from real phishing campaigns, by a student who studied them.
- **Proof points:** 22 deterministic rules; zero network calls in the scan path; published research; open reasoning for every finding.

### Elevator Pitches

**One line:** ScanSafe checks where a QR code really goes before you open it, entirely on
your phone.

**Thirty seconds:** QR phishing works because you can't see where a code leads until
you're already there. ScanSafe closes that gap. Point your phone at a code and it decodes
and scores the link against 22 phishing rules on-device — no network, no accounts,
nothing uploaded. You get a plain-English verdict with the actual reason behind it, before
the page ever loads. It came out of cybersecurity research at Grambling State, built from
phishing campaigns that hit real students.

**Two minutes:** *(the above, plus)* Most QR scanners either open links with no check, or
check them by sending every URL you scan to a server — which means using the security tool
costs you the privacy it was supposed to protect. ScanSafe is architecturally incapable of
that: there is no backend to send anything to. The tradeoff is that it reasons about the
link rather than the destination page, so it uses 22 independent heuristics — brand
typosquatting via longest-common-subsequence matching, redirect-wrapper unwrapping,
dangerous URI schemes, free-hosting abuse — each of which contributes a weight to a final
score. Because it's deterministic, every verdict decomposes into the specific rules that
fired, which is what users actually see. That explainability is the product.

### Competitive Differentiation Narrative

The QR scanner market is a race to the bottom: free apps monetised by ads and data, with
"security" as a checkbox claim backed by a cloud blocklist. ScanSafe competes on the two
axes those apps structurally cannot — it works with the network off, and it can tell you
why. Neither is achievable by an app whose business model requires seeing your links.
The research provenance is the trust anchor: this is a tool with a published methodology,
measured error rates, and rules traceable to documented attacks.

## 5. Visual Design

Visual identity — colour tokens, typography, spacing, components, and the do's and
don'ts — lives in `docs/design.md`, with a live human-readable mirror at
`docs/design.html`. Both were derived from screenshots of the working Flask prototype.

The short version for anyone not opening those files: true-black ground, a single neon
green accent that doubles as the SAFE verdict, amber and red completing the verdict
triad, and a hard rule that those three colours mean nothing except a verdict. Prose is
set in the system sans; every URL and rule output is set in monospace, because users need
to compare `paypa1` against `paypal` character by character.
